Consent and erasure

Domain: privacy · Entity: consent-and-erasure

Implementation-independent · reconciled against backend (no screens yet)
Awaiting approval. The erasure rules in section 4 are proposed and await product-owner approval. The financial retention period (8 years) is a default pending counsel confirmation. Both are in force in the software so they can be tried, but neither is a confirmed policy.

1. Purpose

Lets an organisation show, for each person it holds data about, what that person agreed to and when; and honour a request to be forgotten by replacing the person's identifying details with a pseudonym, while keeping the money trail (amounts, dates, receipt numbers and the books) intact for as long as the law requires.

2. Actors

  • Privacy officer with privacy:consent:record — records a consent granted or withdrawn.
  • Anyone with privacy:consent:read — sees a person's current consent and its full history.
  • Privacy officer with privacy:erase — erases a data subject. Usually a senior role; administrators hold it through *.
  • The data subject — a donor, beneficiary or other person kept as a record, or a member of the organisation. They do not act in the system; staff act on their request.

4. Business rules

Consent

  • Consent history is append-only. A change of mind is a new event; earlier events stay.
  • Consent can be recorded only for a record or member that exists in the organisation. Profiles are not subjects: name the member instead.
  • No free text is kept with consent, so the history holds nothing personal and survives erasure.
  • Consent cannot be recorded for a person who has been erased.
  • Consent is per organisation; another organisation never sees it.

Erasure (proposed, awaiting product-owner approval)

  • Who: only with privacy:erase. A member must be suspended first.
  • All or nothing: one erasure is done completely or not at all.
  • Once: asking again returns the first result and changes nothing.
  • The subject's own record (or a member's profile): every personal field is erased. A field is personal when it is text, long text, rich text, email, phone, location, signature, a file, or marked sensitive — unless the field definition says it is not personal. A field of another type (for example a date of birth) is personal when its definition says so. Totals kept from other records are never personal.
  • How a value is erased: a required text, email or phone field gets the pseudonym (an email becomes erased-<code>@erased.invalid); any other erased value is removed. Values that are kept encrypted stay encrypted: a pseudonym is encrypted under the current key, and removed values leave nothing behind, including their lookup hash.
  • Copies in other records: where another record points at the subject and copied a value from one of the erased fields (a donation's donor name, email, phone and PAN), that copy is erased the same way, so the donation shows the same pseudonym as the donor.
  • Kept: amounts, dates, record numbers, statuses, choices, links between records, totals, and fields marked not personal.
  • Files in erased file fields are detached and removed by the regular file clean-up.
  • History: earlier change history of the erased records keeps who changed what and when, but the erased fields' old values are replaced by "[erased]".
  • Messages about the erased records lose their address, subject and body.
  • Consent: every purpose currently granted is withdrawn, with source "erasure".
  • Members: when the person belongs to no other organisation, their account's name and email are pseudonymised, the account is switched off, sign-in links and sessions end and provider sign-in links are removed. When they also belong to another organisation, the account is left as it is, because it is not this organisation's to erase.
  • Audit: the erasure itself is recorded, naming the fields erased but never their values.

What stays, because the books may not change

  • Issued receipts and posted journals can never be changed or deleted. Receipts hold no donor details themselves; they point to the donation, which is pseudonymised, so a reprinted receipt shows the pseudonym.
  • The description of a posted journal entry keeps the text it was posted with. The donations module writes "Donation <number> from <donor name>", so the donor's name remains in that description after erasure.
  • Payment provider event payloads are kept as received and may contain the payer's email or phone.
  • Data copied by hand into records that do not point at the subject (a donation typed without choosing the donor) is not found and not erased.
  • Background job items, import files still kept, outgoing connector deliveries and archived audit files are not changed.

Retention of financial records (period pending counsel confirmation)

  • Financial records are never deleted by retention or by erasure. The database already refuses to delete posted journals and issued receipts.
  • The retention period is 8 years after the record's date by default, reflecting common Indian statutory practice for books of account. Pending counsel confirmation. Each installation can set its own period.
  • Each erasure reports how many receipts, journal entries and payments it kept, and the date until which they must be kept.
  • No step yet deletes financial records after the period ends.

5. User flows

Find / view

A privacy officer looks up a person by kind and id and sees, for each purpose, the current state, and below it every event in order.

Create

Recording consent: choose the person, the purpose, granted or withdrawn, and where it came from.

Update

Consent is never edited; a new event replaces the current state.

Other actions

Erasure: suspend a member first if the person is a member; then erase, giving a reason (the person asked, consent withdrawn, or other). The result lists the records and fields erased and the financial records kept.

7. Out of scope / non-goals

  • Screens for consent and erasure (the web app has none yet).
  • Deleting financial records once their retention period ends.
  • Erasing an account shared with other organisations.
  • Rewriting posted journal descriptions or provider payment payloads.
  • Requests from the person themselves without staff (self-service).
